Welcome, I am a Ph.D. candidate in Finance at Arizona State University's W. P. Carey School of Business.

My research starts from classic questions in corporate finance. I use unstructured data to revisit long-standing questions that have been difficult to answer with conventional data, with a particular interest in how firms make decisions and grow.

My work studies firms through the perspectives and interactions of people around them, from managers setting strategic direction, to employees working within the organization, to market participants evaluating firms from the outside.

Working Papers

  • Relational Capital Inside the Firm: Evidence from Bilateral Measurement
    With Sunwoo Hwang  ·  Working Paper, February 2026
    Best Paper Award, Conference on Asia-Pacific Financial Markets 2023 and CERFAS 2023
    [SSRN]
    We study labor-management relational capital as a sub-component of corporate culture. Using matched employee-manager survey responses on key relational dimensions to measure an interpretation wedge between the two groups and the survey’s random-sampling design for identification, we find that establishments where employees view relations more favorably than managers subsequently achieve higher profitability, labor productivity, and employee retention. Neither employees’ nor managers’ assessments alone predict these outcomes. The predictive content resides in wedges in relational culture, not in gaps in formal policies or observed disputes, consistent with an interpretation-based theory of relational contracts.
  • Reasoning in Debt Valuation
    Biwon Lee (solo-authored)  ·  Working Paper, November 2025
    [SSRN]
    Using sell-side debt reports on corporate bonds, I examine the underlying reasoning behind financial professionals’ debt valuations. By prompting large language models on 13,879 reports, I identify recommendation-related topics, their associated entities, valuation channels, and sentiment. The main findings indicate that (i) debt analysts' topic attention is primarily focused on balance sheet and income statement items. (ii) The attention exhibits time-varying patterns, with increasing attention to cash and declining attention to profitability during the global financial crisis. (iii) The reasoning analysts employ systematically differs across credit rating categories: discussions of investment-grade issuers emphasize credit ratings, whereas those of high-yield issuers focus on financial fundamentals. (iv) Lastly, quantifying the texts from reports into a vector space using an embedding model, I show that analysts' information can explain bond returns beyond traditional bond-related predictors.
